# Results

## The setup, in one paragraph

An **activation** is a language model's internal state at one word — 3,840
numbers, unreadable directly. The NLA is two models: the **AV** looks at an
activation and writes two sentences describing it; the **AR** reads only those
two sentences and tries to rebuild the original activation. The worry — which the
NLA paper raises itself — is that the AV, being a full language model, might
invent plausible-sounding detail. You cannot catch that by checking AV against
AR, because they were trained together and can agree on a fiction. So this uses a
**sparse autoencoder (SAE)** as an independent witness: it breaks any activation
into a list of active **latents**, and it never saw the NLA.

Three groups of latents matter throughout:

| | |
|---|---|
| **shared** | in the original activation **and** in the AR's rebuild — survived the round trip |
| **lost** | in the original, gone from the rebuild |
| **made** | in the rebuild only — the AR produced it from nowhere visible |

**All numbers below: 200 activations, one explanation each, from 200 distinct
conversations**, on Gemma-3-12B-IT layer 32, sampled from Gemma Scope's own
shipped corpus (the distribution the SAE was fine-tuned on).

> All figures come from `results/SUMMARY.md`, regenerated by
> `src/summarize_results.py`. Quote from that file, not from here.

Method: **[METHODOLOGY.md](METHODOLOGY.md)** · Raw data: [`results/`](.) ·
Experiments that failed their own controls: **[INCONCLUSIVE.md](INCONCLUSIVE.md)**

> Two SAEs are used. A denser one (`l0_big`, ~120 active latents) for §1, where
> the question is how faithfully something can be rebuilt. A sparser one
> (`l0_small`, ~21) from §2 onward, wherever a latent has to be *named* — labels
> generated at `l0_big` fail validation. They are separately trained, so a result
> from one does not automatically hold for the other.

## 1. What the AR produces is not shaped like a real activation

**FVE** measures how close a rebuild is to the original — 1.0 is perfect.

| | what is being rebuilt, by what | FVE |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | a **real activation**, by the SAE | 0.562 |
| **C** | the **AR's output**, by the same SAE | **0.697** |
| **B** | a real activation, by the NLA round trip | 0.691 |
| **D** | both lossy steps chained together | 0.444 |

**C beats A by +0.135, and that is the finding here.** Hand the SAE something the
AR produced and it decomposes it *better* than it decomposes a genuine
activation — using **fewer latents** to do it, 97.0 against 119.2. The AR does not
emit a typical point in activation space. It emits something cleaner, sitting
more squarely inside the SAE's dictionary than the real thing does.

That is a claim about **what the AR emits**, which is why it is the more
interesting of the two comparisons: it characterises the object rather than
ranking two rebuilders. It also has a direct consequence for §2 — when we compare
"what is in the original" against "what is in the rebuild", **the instrument is
sharper on one side than the other**, and the asymmetry favours finding latents
in the rebuild.

**B beats A by +0.129** — an activation survives being written into English and
read back better than it survives a purpose-built 16,384-latent decomposition,
measured on the SAE's home turf with the *stronger* of the two SAEs. Treat this
as the weaker of the two claims: it ranks two systems rather than characterising
either, and per [METHODOLOGY §2](METHODOLOGY.md) the corpus may be in-sample for
the SAE, which flatters row A and makes the comparison conservative rather than
generous.

> **Both gaps are small in cosine terms, and the mean is not the typical case.**
> Gemma's activations are so alike that `rawvar` ≈ 0.028, making
> `FVE = 1 − 68×(1−cos)`; the C − A gap is about 0.0020 of cosine. In the other
> direction the same multiplier means a handful of bad reconstructions dominate
> the mean: row B's **median is +0.762** against a mean of +0.691, with the
> published Gemma figure at 0.768. The typical activation reconstructs at the
> published level and only 2 of 200 pairs fall below zero. `SUMMARY.md` prints
> the full distribution. C > A was measured only on the denser SAE.

## 2. Most latents survive the round trip — and that is not chance

The same 200 activations, re-encoded under each SAE:

| | `l0_small` (~21/activation) | | `l0_big` (~120/activation) | |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| | **count** | **share** | **count** | **share** |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| **shared** | 2,834 | 56.7% | 13,191 | 43.9% |
| **lost** | 1,202 | 24.1% | 10,643 | 35.4% |
| **made** | 959 | 19.2% | 6,205 | 20.7% |

On its own, "56.7% shared" proves nothing — **some latents fire on almost any
text** (punctuation, common grammar), so any two activations overlap a bit for
free. The test is to run the identical comparison against a **completely
unrelated** activation and see what that scores.

**Jaccard** = how much two sets share, divided by everything they cover between
them. 0 means nothing in common, 1 means identical.

| Jaccard | `l0_small` | `l0_big` |
|---|---:|---:|
| rebuild vs **its own** activation | **0.572** | **0.441** |
| rebuild vs an **unrelated** activation | 0.006 | 0.025 |
| ratio | **100×** | **18×** |

Unrelated activations share essentially nothing. **This is the most trustworthy
number in the project**: it is counting matching ID numbers, with no language
model anywhere in the measurement and nothing to calibrate.

## 3. Does the explanation talk about the latents that survive?

**Two separate measurements with two separate nulls.**

### 3a. Bucketing — set arithmetic, no judge

| bucket | per activation | |
|---|---:|---|
| **shared** | 14.2 | fires in the original **and** the rebuild |
| **lost** | 6.0 | fires in the original only |
| **made** | 4.8 | fires in the rebuild only |

`shared + lost = F_orig`, the latents genuinely in the activation — 20.2 per
activation. Its null is §2's mismatched-activation control.

### 3b. Matching — a judge reads the text

The judge is shown one latent's label and one explanation and asked whether the
explanation conveys it. It is run on the **two segments** of each explanation
(`no_final` = everything before the final-token paragraph, `final_only` = that
paragraph) and `full` is reconstructed as their union, so coverage cannot shrink
when text is added. See [METHODOLOGY.md](METHODOLOGY.md) §4.

Over **F_orig** — the same 1,834 latent-judgements for every variant:

| | conveyed | of `shared` | of `lost` |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| **full** | **43.0%** | 47.4% | 30.4% |
| `final_only` | 41.6% | 46.0% | 27.5% |
| `no_final` | 19.6% | 21.6% | 12.8% |

Judge false-positive rate **4.6%**; corrected for it, `full` conveys **40.3%**
of F_orig.

**`made`, reported separately** — these were never in the activation, so they are
not part of faithfulness to it: 866 latents, **37.1%** traceable to the text.

### Is the shared-vs-lost difference real?

Computed within each activation, then compared across activations (never pooled
over pairs, which would overstate confidence ~2.5x):

| | difference | 95%% CI | t | n acts |
|---|---:|---|---:|---:|
| shared vs lost | **+16.5 pts** | [+10.7, +22.3] | **5.53** | 168 |

At 50 activations this comparison was +8.5 points with an interval crossing zero;
the power calculation put the minimum at ~112 independent activations.

> **58%% of `shared` latents are still never mentioned.** `SHARED` means the round
> trip preserved it, not that the explanation said it.

## 4. Which part of the explanation carries it

The AV writes to a stable three-part shape, in 200/200 explanations:

1. what kind of document this is
2. what it is about
3. **what the final token is doing**

Each explanation is generated **once** and then cut, so all three variants
describe the same activation from the same sampled text — the comparison is
paired, and none of the difference comes from resampling the AV.

| variant | tokens | FVE | median | Jaccard | shared | lost | made |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| **full** | 134 | **+0.691** | +0.762 | 0.572 | 14.2 | 6.0 | 4.8 |
| `final_only` (part 3) | 73 | **+0.582** | +0.670 | 0.494 | 11.9 | 8.3 | 4.2 |
| `no_final` (parts 1–2) | 59 | **+0.268** | +0.333 | 0.250 | 6.1 | 14.1 | 5.2 |

Paired per activation, `final_only` − `no_final` is **+0.314 FVE** (t = 15.5),
**+0.163 Jaccard** (t = 20.4) and **+5.8 shared latents** (t = 19.5).

### The four measures agree

Each measure as a fraction of `full`. Three of these are independent
instruments — vector geometry, integer set overlap through an SAE that never saw
the NLA, and an LLM reading text:

| measure | `final_only` | `no_final` |
|---|---:|---:|
| FVE (AR reconstruction) | **0.84** | **0.39** |
| Jaccard (SAE latent overlap) | **0.86** | **0.44** |
| shared latents recovered | **0.84** | **0.43** |
| judged: conveyed of F_orig | **0.97** | **0.46** |

`final_only` spans 0.84–0.97 across all four; `no_final` spans 0.39–0.46.

**What the agreement rules out.** Had `final_only` scored high on FVE and low on
the SAE and judge measures, that would indicate the final paragraph carries
something the AR can reconstruct from without it corresponding to content a
reader recovers — and FVE would be a poor proxy for meaning in this NLA. The
measured direction is the opposite: `final_only`'s judged ratio (0.97) is
*higher* than its FVE ratio (0.84).

**`made` barely moves across variants** — 4.8 / 4.2 / 5.2 per activation. Cutting
the explanation down changes how much the AR recovers, not how much it invents.

### Stated limitations

- The split is anchored on the paragraph naming the final token. Measured over
  250 explanations, that phrase appears in exactly one paragraph, and never the
  first, in 250/250. On this run the anchor succeeded 200/200.
- `no_final` and `final_only` are close in length (59 vs 73 tokens), so the
  difference between them is not a length effect. `full` is 134.
- Where the three-part format comes from is not tested here.

## Limitations

- **200 activations, one model, one layer, one corpus.** One activation per
  conversation and one explanation per activation, so 200 is also the number of
  independent samples.
- **The corpus may be in-sample for the SAE.** Google documents neither where
  `examples.safetensors` came from nor whether the SAE was trained on it. If it
  was, §1's row A is flattering to the SAE, which makes the NLA comparison
  harder to win rather than easier. See [METHODOLOGY.md](METHODOLOGY.md) §2.
- **The judge's false-negative rate is not measurable at scale.** The SAE gives
  free ground truth for false positives — a latent either fired or it did not —
  but nothing establishes that an explanation *did* mean to mention something.
  A 40-pair hand audit of an earlier version found it missed 8 and over-called 0.
- **`full`'s coverage is a union of its two segments**, so it inherits both
  segments' false positives (FPR 7.0% against 1.6% and 5.4%) and misses any
  latent conveyed only by the two paragraphs *combined*. It is a lower bound.
- **Only half the latents can be named**, and the unnamed half may behave
  differently.
- **An SAE does not have a latent for everything.** A claim can be true with no
  latent to match it, so a missing latent is weak evidence of absence — a limit
  the NLA paper notes about its own method.
- **Nothing here separates "the AR was told" from "the AR guessed well".**
  Doing so needs an AR trained independently of the AV, which the released
  checkpoints do not provide.
- **The SAE reads the rebuild more clearly than the original** (§1, C > A), so
  `made` counts are not a like-for-like comparison.
- **Nothing here checks whether a latent is *true* of the source text.** Both
  attempts failed their own controls — [INCONCLUSIVE.md](INCONCLUSIVE.md).
- **The confabulation finding is not new.** The NLA paper documents it. What is
  new here is checking it against SAE latents.
